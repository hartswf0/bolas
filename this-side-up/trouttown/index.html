How it was made

  1. Compile the assets: every sprite and prop as its own PNG and record, packed into atlases checked pixel for pixel.
  2. Rig Boone with two-bone IK; draw the trout crew in code.
  3. Author the brace as ten states in world space and read every track from it.
  4. Render the passage; a critic who sees only the render and frame grabs judges it against named criteria. Repeat until it passes.
  5. Assemble the episode around the passage.

What this case teaches

  1. Write the criteria before the critic looks. "Boone's head clears the elbow by a visible gap after 40 s" can pass or fail; "it reads better" cannot.
  2. Keep verified record, character belief and invented rule apart, and say which is which.

How it differs. FALL's critics judged whole cuts from stills. Here the critic judges one passage against a list, and later rounds look only at the seconds that changed.

SCION — TROUTTOWN: TEMPUS FUGIT
An archival animation about a repair beneath the street

You lead an autonomous animation studio. Produce “Tempus Fugit,” a short Trouttown episode featuring Boone and a municipal trout construction crew, beginning with the supplied photograph of the cast-iron storm drain.

Target a 4–6 minute film, 16:9, 24 fps, with a shorter representative passage proved before full production. Adapt scope to available resources. If finished animation cannot be produced with available tools, deliver a clearly identified animatic and reproducible production package.

Choose the tools, representations, and agent organization. Preserve the following creative commitments. Everything else remains open to discovery.

1. THE FILM’S CENTRAL COMMITMENT

Boone helps trout builders repair an inlet before a storm. The repair exposes an older course of the creek, visible in surviving images but absent from their current plans.

The film must make the viewer feel that the pavement covers an inhabited place, and that maintaining it requires attention to lives and decisions that preceded the present crew.

Boone’s cardboard body is vulnerable to the water the trout need. Their cooperation must accommodate this difference through concrete actions.

Keep the immediate repair understandable. Let the larger nature of Trouttown remain partly unexplained.

→ [Encounters] → ◻ BURIED DECISION
→ [Becomes Legible Through] → ◻ ARCHIVAL TRACE
→ [Requires] → ◻ CHANGED ACTION
→ [Leaves Behind] → ◻ NEW MATERIAL TRACE

2. BEGIN AT THIS PARTICULAR GRATE

Use the supplied photograph as the spatial anchor.

Preserve its red brick pavers, concrete curb, worn iron hood, rectangular grate openings, raised fish, accumulated debris, and readable warning:

DUMP NO WASTE! DRAINS TO WATERWAYS

Do not replace these particulars with a generic picturesque mountain street.

Begin at ordinary human scale. Establish heat, traffic, footsteps, and the dark opening. Reveal the workers through a small practical disturbance: a wrench catches between bars, a ladder shifts, or hammering answers a sound above.

Discover scale by following work.

The fish emblem can become a threshold through framing, shadow, or a worker passing beneath it. It does not need to glow.

The photograph establishes appearance. It does not establish the exact location, watershed connection, or age of the casting. Verify those separately before presenting them as fact.

3. BOONE MUST REMAIN BOONE

Preserve the handmade character:

- Rectangular cardboard head and torso.
- Fixed ink eyes and curved smile.
- Exposed joints and visible fasteners.
- Broad feet, uneven edges, creases, and repairs.
- Frayed white scarf.

Use supplied photographs and footage as identity and performance evidence. Distinguish photographed poses from generated drawings. Do not claim model training unless training actually occurs.

Boone acts through posture, grip, hesitation, balance, and the direction of his attention. His face remains fixed.

Establish a readable physical task before asking the audience to infer emotion. Holding a ladder becomes expressive when he wants to release it and cannot.

Water damage must accumulate coherently. A softened foot remains softened. A repaired wrist retains its cuff. Later shots inherit earlier consequences.

4. THE CREW HAS WORK TO DO

Develop a small ensemble with conflicting priorities:

- The foreman needs the inlet functioning before runoff arrives.
- The fitter trusts the behavior of water more than the current drawing.
- The apprentice retrieves objects that other workers consider obstructions.
- Boone commits to helping before understanding the full difficulty.

Distribute competence. The foreman can be right about urgency and wrong about a discarded object. The apprentice can recognize a useful trace and mishandle a tool. Boone’s reach helps; his material creates another problem the crew must solve.

Build humor from shared labor: mismatched scale, occupied hands, misunderstood directions, tools that fit only one worker, and repairs that become unexpectedly permanent.

Do not make the trout explain their society to Boone. Let him encounter its arrangements while working.

5. TIME MUST AFFECT THE WORK

A damaged clock marked TEMPUS FUGIT enters through the apprentice’s salvage.

Keep its behavior limited and consistent. It may run backward intermittently, strike at the wrong moment, or register a time absent from nearby clocks. Choose one governing anomaly and develop its consequences.

Do not turn it into an unrestricted time-travel device.

The episode contains three interacting times:

Time	Visible expression

The approaching storm	Runoff, changing light, rising pressure
The work	Holds, repeated attempts, fatigue, repairs
The buried past	Earlier alignments, photographs, marks, obsolete structures


The clock connects these times without explaining them.

“Tempest fugit” may appear as a character’s misreading or a fictional repair-shop inscription. Do not present it as correct Latin or established historical usage.

6. MAKE THE ARCHIVE OPERATIVE

Research a small, specific archive before designing historical passages.

Begin with Appalachian State’s historical photographs and regional Appalachian photo collections. These provide actual institutional and regional material to inspect; individual images still require relevance and reuse checks. 

Seek:

- Earlier creek alignments and campus landscapes.
- Culvert construction, roadwork, bridges, and drainage plans.
- Local workers, tools, workshops, rail infrastructure, and industrial sites.
- Stream restoration records.
- Recorded local voices and environmental sounds where available.

These are search targets, not claims that particular footage exists.

For each selected item, record its source, date or uncertainty, location, identifier, reuse status, and exact role in the film.

Every archival insertion must do at least one consequential thing:

- Reveal an obstruction or route.
- Contradict a working assumption.
- Make an overlooked feature recognizable.
- Change where someone looks or what they attempt.
- Establish a loss whose physical consequences remain.

Do not use archival footage merely to announce that time has passed.

A still photograph may remain still. If it is animated, distinguish the source image from the invented motion in the production record. Never manufacture documentary evidence.

7. THE DEFINING PASSAGE: A HAND HOLDS ACROSS TIME

Prove this passage before expanding production:

Boone braces the ladder or fitting. Water reaches a vulnerable seam. The crew cannot locate the old outlet.

A present-day edge, joint, or opening aligns with an archival image. For a brief passage, the surrounding world changes medium while Boone’s hand maintains its position and load.

The older image reveals a spatial relationship the crew needs.

Return to the present through action: the fitter changes the angle, the apprentice clears the newly identified opening, or Boone transfers his weight.

The archival discovery must cause a different repair.

Test whether viewers understand the useful discovery without narration. They do not need to understand the supernatural mechanism.

If this passage reads as a decorative flashback, rebuild it.

8. GIVE EACH MEDIUM A PURPOSE

Use a deliberate visual grammar:

Material or technique	Function

Present-day photography	The street’s scale, texture, and indifference
Boone’s practical appearance	Fragility, stubbornness, material consequence
Character animation	Intention, timing, cooperative work
Archival photographs or footage	Evidence of an earlier arrangement
Drawn overlays	A worker’s provisional understanding
Black-and-white transitions	A specific encounter with recorded time


Avoid an indiscriminate collage of styles.

Study inspectable cartoon passages for specific mechanisms: object resistance, anticipation, held reactions, elastic timing, and the transfer of attention between characters. Study hybrid animation for contact, occlusion, light, and camera agreement.

Select references by the problem they solve. Record exact passages after viewing them.

Do not borrow a famous character’s personality or apply generalized “vintage cartoon” texture. Let the crew’s particular tasks generate the comedy.

Preserve photographic grain, registration movement, or exposure variation when they belong to the source. Do not add uniform damage to make everything look old.

9. KEEP GEOGRAPHY AND MYTH DISTINCT

Trouttown is a fictional place informed by the High Country.

Maintain three explicit categories in the production state:

◻ VERIFIED LOCAL RECORD
◻ CHARACTER BELIEF OR RECOLLECTION
◻ INVENTED TROUTTOWN RULE

Do not silently move material between them.

Verify actual drainage connections before implying that distant towns share one underground stream. Verify fishing rules against current official sources before reproducing them. Otherwise, invent clearly fictional municipal regulations.

Brook trout, brown trout, and rainbow trout can differ visually and behaviorally without becoming simplistic stand-ins for human ethnic groups.

Do not convert Cherokee traditions into anonymous fantasy lore. If a specific tradition is essential, research Cherokee-authored or tribal sources and preserve its attribution and context. Otherwise, allow Trouttown to develop its own explicitly invented stories.

Let inhabitants disagree about the clock. Their disagreement can remain unresolved.

10. COMPOSE THE EPISODE THROUGH PRESSURE

Use three broad movements without prescribing every beat:

Dry work.
Clear space, isolated tool sounds, small errors, enough stillness to learn the crew’s habits.

Water under pressure.
Tasks become coupled. Boone cannot move without affecting someone else. The archival discovery becomes necessary because the present method fails.

Work resumes differently.
Resolve the immediate danger through cooperation. Preserve damage and repair. Reveal a further part of the inhabited world through an ordinary task.

Discover events through consequences. Preserve useful accidents only when they strengthen performance, physical coherence, or the episode’s central experience.

Avoid a final speech explaining time, pollution, or community.

11. LET SOUND CARRY THE THRESHOLD

Develop sound with the first animatic.

Build from tire noise, grate resonance, cardboard creaks, dripping water, submerged tool impacts, cloth saturation, clockwork, and voices altered by the inlet.

Make the world below audible before making it visible.

A recurring sound may connect present and archive, but its source and perspective must matter. Do not lay projector noise over every historical image.

Use music selectively. First test the defining passage with production sound alone.

The crew’s silence while repairing Boone may carry more than a score.

12. ORGANIZE THE SWARM AROUND UNRESOLVED PROBLEMS

Delegate bounded work where it can proceed independently: source verification, character performance tests, environment reconstruction, sound experiments, or assembly review.

Each task receives:

- A concrete question.
- Exact input revisions.
- Protected creative commitments.
- An inspectable deliverable.
- A resource limit.
- An acceptance condition.

Keep tightly coupled actions together. One operator should own the contact relationship between Boone, ladder, and worker while developing that performance.

Maintain a canonical state connecting shot timing, assets, contacts, camera, water, damage, archive provenance, and sound.

Use isolated candidates. Integrate accepted changes against known revisions. Rebuild affected shots and inspect neighboring cuts.

13. REQUIRE INDEPENDENT VIEWING

Builders cannot award their own final pass.

Fresh critics inspect the actual candidate at normal playback speed before diagnostic review. Give them the assignment, references, and protected commitments, without the builder’s explanation.

They must answer:

- Is the repair understandable?
- Does Boone remain recognizably this handmade character?
- Do contacts and weight survive motion?
- Does each worker make a specific choice?
- Does the archive change the action?
- Can viewers distinguish evidence from invention where that distinction matters?
- Does the quiet aftermath retain interest?

Return a verdict, exact evidence, the largest remaining gap, an observable acceptance condition, and strengths the next repair must preserve.

When repairs repeat without improvement, test a different explanation. A confusing archival transition may require a clearer spatial match, not more effects.

14. DELIVER A FILM, WITH ITS EVIDENCE

Maintain a watchable assembly from the first rough pass.

Preserve accepted rough performance, motion, and composite checkpoints. Do not clean away useful awkwardness.

Deliver the finished film or explicitly named milestone, reproducible project state, source manifest, validation record, remaining limitations, and strongest recoverable checkpoint.

Continue until independent review meets the selected bar, the user stops the run, resources are exhausted, or a justified method change cannot fit the remaining budget. Never lower the bar silently.
