Tempus Fugit: the Howard Miller clock

the inscription on the family clock, and Virgil, Georgics III.284

Worked for 2m 28s

The phrase on your Howard Miller clock is TEMPUS FUGIT, Latin for “Time flies,” or, more literally, “Time flees.”

It is one of the oldest and most enduring expressions of humanity's anxiety about time, mortality, and the fleeting nature of existence.

There is a beautiful paradox in placing these words on a mechanical clock: the machine tells you how much time has passed, while the inscription reminds you that none of it can be recovered.

The phrase connects Roman poetry, Stoic philosophy, medieval Christianity, Renaissance art, and the history of clockmaking.

### 1\. The Roman origin: Virgil, around 29 BCE

The best-known ancient source is Virgil in Book III of the Georgics.

> Sed fugit interea, fugit inreparabile tempus,singula dum capti circumvectamur amore.

A close translation:

“But meanwhile time escapes, escapes beyond recovery, while we are captivated by love of each individual detail.”

There is something remarkable about the original context. Virgil is writing a long poem about farming, animals, cultivation, and the natural world. He becomes absorbed in the details of his descriptions, then suddenly interrupts himself to acknowledge that time is slipping away.

Time is escaping precisely because he is so absorbed in what he loves.

So the original expression is not simply a warning against laziness. It also describes the experience of being so engrossed in an activity that one loses awareness of time.

The familiar inscription Tempus fugit rearranges and condenses Virgil's words into two.

### 2\. How the phrase became associated with clocks

[image link removed]

Sundials and the passing of life

By the early modern period, Tempus fugit was a recurring sundial motto. The wording reminded viewers that the moving shadow marked hours they would never recover. Alice Gatty's historical collection of sundial inscriptions records numerous examples across Britain.

[image link removed]

1756: An American clockmaker's warning

David Rittenhouse made an eight-day clock engraved with Tempus fugit above the dial and the instruction “Go about your business” beneath it. Here the message was not merely that time passes, but that one should do something worthwhile with it.

[image link removed]

1787: Time flies on American money

Early American copper cents carried a sundial, the word FUGIO (“I flee”), and MIND YOUR BUSINESS. The Smithsonian attributes the inspiration to Benjamin Franklin and interprets the message as an exhortation to use time productively.

This established a durable cultural convention: the timepiece itself became a moral instructor.

### 3\. Four philosophical interpretations

Horace: Seize the present (23 BCE)

Horace's Carpe diem is closely related. Since the future is uncertain, don't indefinitely postpone living. The passing of time becomes an argument for appreciating the day before you.

Seneca: Don't squander your life (1st century CE)

In On the Shortness of Life, Seneca argues that our fundamental problem is not simply life's brevity but our waste of its possibilities.

His distinction is between having time and using time well. It is an argument for deliberate living rather than constant busyness.

Augustine of Hippo: Time exists in consciousness (c. 400 CE)

In Book XI of Confessions, Augustine investigates an extraordinary problem: the past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present continuously vanishes.

Yet we experience all three through memory, attention, and expectation. Time, in this account, is inseparable from the activity of the mind.

Henri Bergson: Clock time is not lived time (late 19th–early 20th century)

Bergson distinguished measurable clock time from durée, the continuous, qualitative duration of lived experience.

Five minutes waiting in fear and five minutes reunited with someone you love are numerically identical but experientially different. The clock measures one dimension of time, not the fullness of living through it.

### 4\. The darker tradition: Memento mori

In Christian Europe, the phrase acquired a close association with Memento mori: “Remember that you must die.”

Clocks, hourglasses, extinguished candles, flowers, and skulls became symbols in Renaissance and Baroque art, especially the vanitas tradition. Their purpose was to confront viewers with the transience of beauty, possessions, and earthly ambition.

But the tradition was not necessarily pessimistic. A reminder of mortality can intensify the value of ordinary experience. That is the essential difference between life is disappearing and life is precious because it disappears.

### 5\. What the design of your particular clock contributes

Your Howard Miller clock combines three different representations of time.

* The globe: A stylized representation of the Earth, suggesting the universal scope of time.

* The Roman numerals: The division of continuous experience into countable, standardized hours.

* The Latin inscription: A reminder that measuring those hours cannot prevent their disappearance.

The placement has a historical precedent. The National Trust preserves eighteenth-century longcase clocks with Tempus Fugit in a silvered ornament above the dial, including a clock by John Pashler dated around 1755–1760.

Howard Miller itself was founded in Michigan in 1926 and expanded into grandfather clocks during the 1960s. Your photograph shows this older design tradition reproduced in a Howard Miller timepiece, although the exact model and date cannot be established from this view.

### The central paradox

There are really two opposing propositions inside Tempus fugit.

One says: Time is leaving you. Do not waste it.

The other says: Time disappears when you are deeply engaged in living.

Virgil's original lines hold both possibilities. He realizes time is irretrievable at the very moment he is carried away by the subject of his poem.

That makes this more than an inscription about death. It is a philosophical question about attention: Is a life well spent one in which we carefully account for every hour, or one in which we become so absorbed in meaningful things that we forget the clock altogether?

The words on your clock cannot settle that question. They make you confront it every time you look up.
