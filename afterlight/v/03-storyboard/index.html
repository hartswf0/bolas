Each layer is labelled PHOTOGRAPHED or INVENTED, figures are matted over plates, and captions sit on paper slips.

Suggested medium: multiplane cut paper with a warm interior, after Norstein-style layered depth. Low sun through a window and dust in the beam. The stone and the hands carry the drama. Do not make the stone glow orange to signal heat; temperature must read from hands and posture.

  4. The child touches the sill stone, withdraws, touches it again, and recognises it.
  5. The child carries the stone wrapped in cloth and places it in the waiting hands, which close around it. End on the hands.

  • Archive footage of sunsets through windows, hands, hand mirrors and domestic interiors. Try Internet Archive / Prelinger and movingimagearchive.com.

Medium: embossed cut-paper relief on a tabletop under one lamp. The picture is computed as a height field, and its spread mimics heat diffusion: thin branches stay shallow and the solid sun balloons. It is then shaded by the actual lamp position, so raking light and no light look clearly different.

Cast: two equal makers.

  • A: ochre sleeve, darker skin.
  • B: slate sleeve, lighter skin.
  • In KF4 the roles cross: A reads by touch and B revises the print.

Beats:

  1. Lamp on. A draws; B traces with eyes closed and finds nothing.
  2. B rejects the thin branches. A spreads paste on the back of a reprint with thicker lines while B holds the corner.
  3. Under raking light the sun over-swells, the hill reads clearly and the branches barely rise. Both wait the 30 s cool-down from the tutorial.
  4. Both read the relief by touch. B stipples the sun on the next print so it will rise less.
  5. Lamp off, cool ambient light. Two fingers follow the hill from opposite ends toward each other.

Research basis (verified):

  • The paper: Sosuke Ichihashi, Noura Howell and HyunJoo Oh, "Swell by Light: An Approachable Technique for Freeform Raised Textures", TEI '25 (ACM), Article 45, pp. 1–16, March 2025, DOI 10.1145/3689050.3704420. Ichihashi is the first author.
  • Lab page: https://sites.gatech.edu/futurefeelings/?p=474
  • Department record: https://lmc.gatech.edu/publications/pub/7751
  • What it claims: a dark pattern is printed on the front of the paper and a heat-expanding paste is spread on the back. Visible light heats the dark areas and the back swells. The paper includes a print-pattern optimisation algorithm based on thermal simulation: it darkens thin lines and lightens large solid areas to compensate for heat dissipation. Its applications are tactile picture books, elevation maps, touch and pressure sensors, scented braille and textured origami.
  • Tutorials: the lab's tutorials add that "thin lines don't puff well", that the light should be 100 W or more (or sunlight through a magnifier), and that bumps need about 30 s to cool before touching.

Correction to the treatment: the heat-diffusion work is not separate "accompanying work". It is part of the same paper, as the thermal-simulation optimisation. I found no separate heat-diffusion paper. Suggested replacement wording: "Swell by Light (Ichihashi, Howell & Oh, TEI 2025) raises textures by shining light through a dark printed pattern onto a heat-expanding paste; the same paper uses thermal simulation to adjust the print so that heat spreading through the sheet does not leave thin lines shallow or over-swell solid areas." The treatment's own note that the collaboration is fiction is correct and is kept in archivePlan.

References I could not verify:

  • ACM DOI page: dl.acm.org refused the fetcher. The paper's details are confirmed through the lab page, the LMC record and Semantic Scholar instead. It is marked verified:false in basis.
  • Not used: the Vimeo video (I could only reach a verification screen), the Perkins Flickr tactile-map album (found no album or licence), the Library of Congress item pages (failed to load), the David Rumsey item pages (blocked by robots) and the American Printing House museum records (redirect to another site).

Rights to check before any plate is used:

  • Archive footage: the only public-domain film I found is Prelinger "Work Pays America" (1937), whose shot list includes braille maps being prepared and read. "Instructing the Blind Worker" (1944) is also public domain but shows only drill-press training. The NFB stock shot 3493 (hands reading Braille, extreme close-up), BFI "Braille Lesson" (1962) and AFB "Communicating with Deaf-Blind People" (1964) are marked "unknown — not granted" and used as references only.
  • Stills: the public-domain LOC/Bain "Map of Europe, for blind" and "Making Matrix", the LOC Red Cross 1918 "Learning to read and write by touch", and NLM and Florida Memory Flickr Commons photos.
  • Objects: the Howe 1837 atlas is CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 on the Rumsey site. For Smithsonian NMAH 600011 (Poates 1919 relief map) the metadata is CC0 but image reuse is restricted.
  • movingimagearchive.com: I found no tactile or braille footage there. Its search page would not load, and the homepage lists none.

Research basis (confirmed): Sosuke Ichihashi, Masahiko Inami, Hsin-Ni Ho and Noura Howell, "Hydroptical Thermal Feedback: Spatial Thermal Feedback Using Visible Lights and Water," UIST '24, Article 104, pp. 1–19, ACM, October 2024. DOI 10.1145/3654777.3676453 (https://doi.org/10.1145/3654777.3676453). Ichihashi is first author, from Howell's Future Feelings Lab at Georgia Tech. I confirmed this through the GT LMC publication record, the lab's project page and the UIST video. The ACM page itself refused automated fetch.

What the paper claims:

  • Visible light cast on skin under water is perceived as a thermal sensation.
  • Shorter wavelengths gave stronger warmth.
  • Brighter light felt warmer; people reacted in about 1.5 s.
  • Switching lights in sequence made warmth and coolness appear to move along the skin.
  • Lighting all the submerged skin raised perceived water temperature by about 13 °C in 25 °C water, which is the "water itself feels warmer" illusion.
  • One body of water can give different people different perceived temperatures. The authors list "shared water experiences" as a proposed application.

Treatment check: The treatment's summary of the paper is accurate. Two cautions to keep:

  • The paper reports perception. It does not claim the water is heated, and the module says so.
  • The two people negotiating together is the film's fiction, not a finding. The treatment already says this.

Other corrections:

  • The brief says "Weber's three-bowl experiment". I found no source that credits E. H. Weber with it. The Franklin Institute and Scientific American versions name no originator. The documented early source is Locke, Essay, Book II ch. VIII: "the same water, at the same time, may produce the idea of cold by one hand and of heat by the other." The module cites Locke and the Franklin Institute activity instead.
  • I confirmed Locke's wording through a concordance and a secondary quotation. I could not read the Gutenberg text as far as Book II, so the section number §21 is unconfirmed.

  • Home Movie: Hot Springs 1943 (Prelinger) is verified, but the item page states no rights.

    • Health: Your Cleanliness (Centron, 1953): covers hand washing and bathing.

    • He placed the heating maximum at "probably not less than half an inch beyond the last visible" red.

In the sources, the standards sat in shade, and the readings past the red came from a deliberate second series of experiments.
