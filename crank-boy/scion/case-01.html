02 · What the future claimed

The card removes the pupil's reading. It keeps the book.

The brief asks whether the image predicts the disappearance of books, the elimination of reading labor, or the preservation of educational authority through a new apparatus. Tested proposition by proposition, it shows the second and third and contradicts the first.

Working verdict (v1, kept after critique). The card depicts the mechanization of reading in service of an unchanged school. The book is not replaced; it becomes fuel, and on the mill reading (C4) it is used up. Of Jang's codes, "obsolesce: reading" holds. "Medium: direct transmission to the brain" names the output end only: the apparatus is better described as a book-consuming broadcast, and the book should be coded as its input. Scope "Transforms practice" holds; "Replaces a medium" would be wrong.

T0: what "Histoire de France" meant in 1910

The Ferry laws of 1881–82 made French primary schooling free, compulsory and secular, and the March 1882 law replaced catechism with moral and civic instruction. Documented

School textbooks were then fought over twice. In 1882 the Congregation of the Index condemned four civics textbooks. A second "textbook war" ran from 1907 to 1914; in a collective letter published in La Croix on 28 September 1909, the French bishops condemned twelve school manuals, nine of them history books. Documented

The documented 1909 condemnation came from the Church against lay manuals used in republican schools. It was not a state inspector swapping one edition for another; the inspector and "approved edition" in the 07 game are invented. Counterfactual

So at the BnF sheet's date, which history book a child received was a live political question. The card shows the book going into the machine as if its contents were settled. No evidence connects the illustrator to the dispute; the point is about what the image leaves out, not about intent. Inferred

What the audience is asked to accept as permanent: that someone above the pupil chooses the edition, that every pupil gets the same one, and that the person doing the physical work is not a learner.

04 · The media tetrad

Primary tetrad: the machine as a reader for the room

The brief's starting hypotheses were tested against the regions. Two changed: what is obsolesced is the pupil's act of reading rather than the book, and what is retrieved is the reading-aloud of one text to a seated group.

Enhance

Simultaneous, identical delivery of one text to many (P2). The selecting adult's control over what everyone receives (P4).

R2, R5

Retrieve

One reader, many listeners: the lectio, the lecture, the recited lesson. The pupils' posture is the posture of being read to.

R4. Brief's hypothesis kept, narrowed.

Obsolesce

The pupil's reading: eyes on the page, pace set by the reader, the ability to go back. Also copying and handwriting. Not the book.

R4, R6, R9. Brief said "reading or reading labor"; the book itself survives.

Reverse

Pushed to the limit, uniform reception removes the means to detect error. If every pupil holds the same sentence, comparing with a classmate confirms only that the machine said it. The class becomes exactly as reliable as the volume in the hopper, and the hopper's owner gains authority.

Derived from P2 + P4. Tested in 07.

Enhance ↔ Obsolesce

The future presented as a natural result of better machinery: faster, uniform schooling with less effort from the pupil. This pairing is the one Jang codes for the card, and the one most claims in her archive state.

Retrieve ↔ Reverse

Read together, they show the less obvious future. A technology that returns to reading-aloud also returns to the authority of the person holding the text. The pupil's own reading was the part of the old arrangement that let a pupil check the master; the machine removes it.

Alternative tetrad: the machine as a press

Enhance

The book's reach. The approved volume now enters twenty heads at once.

Retrieve

Hand-powered manufacture: the crank of the hand press or mill, brought into the classroom.

R7, R8

Obsolesce

The master's voice and explanation. He loads; he does not teach.

R5: his mouth is closed, his hands are busy.

Reverse

The teacher becomes an operator of equipment chosen by others. Cuban found this role, "teacher as technician", in the early decades of classroom television.

Alternative tetrad: the machine as a mill (C4)

Enhance

Quantity: more pages into more heads per hour.

Retrieve

Force-feeding, and the old description of reading as eating and chewing over a text.

R8 form

Obsolesce

Chewing: the slow, repeated reading by which a reader digests a text.

Reverse

Stuffed, not nourished. Content goes in and nothing shows it was taken up; the book is used up in the process.

This tetrad and the primary one agree on obsolescence (the pupil's own reading) and disagree on retrieval. Which one fits depends on evidence outside this scan; see 08.

A further tetrad from reading C2 (satire) is recorded but not developed: enhance passivity, obsolesce attention, retrieve the dunce, reverse into a class that sleeps through its own education. It depends on authorial intent, which is unknown.

Figure and ground

Figure: the blue machine, the wires, the caps.

Ground: the national history volume, the master's gesture, the rows, the boy at the crank, the full shelf.

Inverted: with the machine in the background, the card is a record of who held which role in a 1910 classroom: an old man choosing the text, a working boy powering the lesson, seated children receiving it, at a moment when the choice of history book was disputed. The invention is the least historically specific thing in the picture.

05 · What actually happened

The book coexisted with every medium meant to replace it

T0 is the card's present (1899–1910). T1 is the classroom it shows. T2 is the record since. These comparisons are not a sequence of progress: each medium entered schools on its own terms, and several left again.

Comparisons

WhenClaim or projectWhat followedStatus
1913Edison: books "will soon be obsolete in the public schools" and the school system changed within ten years (New York Dramatic Mirror, 9 July 1913).The trade paper Motion Picture News answered that November that films would supplement school books, not replace them. Print books remain the common format: in October 2025, 64% of US adults had read a print book in the past year, against 31% e-books and 26% audiobooks (Pew).Documented
1924–37Benjamin Darrow's schools of the air: Sears-funded in Chicago (1924), then the Ohio School of the Air from 7 January 1929. Darrow's 1932 book called radio a "textbook of the air" and made the classroom teacher the radio teacher's assistant.The Ohio Department of Education stopped funding it in 1937 (Ohio Memory); the critic reports the university carried it on, not verified here. CBS's American School of the Air followed and expanded abroad. Radio delivered the same lesson to many classrooms at once, which is P2 realized by a different mechanism.Documented
1934–Talking Books: the American Foundation for the Blind put whole books on long-playing records from late 1934, working with the Library of Congress.The closest realization of P1 with books as input: a book's text reaching a listener who does not read the page. Within a year, 27 titles were in circulation. It served readers who could not see the page, and it supplemented print.Documented
1920s–60sTeaching machines: Pressey's testing machine, Skinner's programmed instruction.The machines did not take hold in classrooms; their ideas (small units, individual pacing) moved into textbooks and testing (Watters 2021). Pressey also promoted standardized testing.Documented
1920–86Film, radio, television, microcomputers in classrooms.Cuban (1986) found a repeated cycle: exhilaration, claims of scientific backing, disappointment, then blame on teachers. The technologies were chosen and adopted mostly by non-teachers.Documented
2023–Khanmigo: a GPT-4 tutor piloted by Khan Academy from March 2023; Sal Khan's stated goal is an AI tutor for every student and an assistant for every teacher.Too recent to classify. One design choice runs opposite to the card: Khanmigo is built to withhold answers and ask the student questions.Documented claim
Unknown outcome

Afterlife

The card became widely known after Asimov republished the Côté set in 1986, and online after about 2012. It now circulates mostly as an example of failed or premature ed-tech prediction, including in Jang's archive. No evidence was found that it influenced any educational technology; resemblance to later devices is not treated here as influence.

06 · Three alternative futures

Three changes to the rules, not to the gadget

Each counterfuture starts from a contradiction identified in 02–05 and changes one relationship the card held fixed. All three are Counterfactual and stay out of the historical record.

A · Developed in 07

The book as witness

Source contradiction
Reverse (04) and I9: uniform transmission leaves nothing to point to when two people disagree.
Altered rule
The machine works as drawn, but a transmitted sentence carries no page, edition or author. The printed book becomes the only thing that can be cited. Reading survives as checking.
Actors
Pupil with an older edition; master bound to the approved one; the crank boy who sees the hopper; a neighbour who heard the same sentence; an inspector who decides editions.
Infrastructure
One hopper, one bus, a shelf with two editions of the same title.
Failure modes
Reading becomes evidence-handling for the few who keep old copies. Editions fight through copies. Whoever loads the hopper still decides what the room knows.
Competing readings
Restores the pupil's reading as a right; or turns reading into litigation over editions.
Operations
3 Retrieve the obsolete; 1 Invert the promise.

B

The school dissolves

Source contradiction
I4 and the 1907–14 textbook war: the classroom exists to deliver one edition, and the edition is disputed.
Altered rule
Attendance is no longer compulsory. Hopper machines are cheap; any café, parish, union hall or workshop with a machine and a shelf runs lessons.
Actors
Parish priest, republican schoolmaster, union secretary, factory owner, parents choosing a hopper, children paying by the crank.
Infrastructure
Machines, shelves, licences for editions, crank labor for hire.
Failure modes
Each network transmits its own national history; no shared reference survives; poorer children crank for others' lessons.
Competing readings
Freedom from the state curriculum; or privatized instruction sorted by who owns machines.
Operations
4 Break the invariant; 5 Material conditions.

C

The hopper is open

Source contradiction
P6 and I1: pupils receive and the master selects.
Altered rule
Any pupil may put a book in, interrupt, or retransmit to the bus.
Actors
Pupils with books from home, pupils without, the crank boy who sets the speed, the master as moderator.
Infrastructure
A queue for the hopper; a switch on every desk.
Failure modes
Authority moves from selecting to queueing. The most-fed volume becomes what the class believes. The crank operator becomes the gatekeeper.
Competing readings
A democratic classroom; or one ruled by whoever brings the most books.
Operations
2 Change the actor; 6 Follow an unexpected user.

Critique accepted: all three turn on one axis, control of the input. A fourth axis, the caps and the body, was not taken up. It is recorded as prompt D in 08.

Why A was developed

A tests the competing reading C1 directly (the machine depends on books) and the 1909 dispute (which edition goes in). It fits in one room, so it can be played in a few minutes. B needs a town-scale simulation and is left as a design prompt. C's actions (feed the hopper, interrupt, stop the crank) are included inside A's game, so its central claim can still be reached by play.

Design prompt derived from A

Starting situationA morning lesson in the illustrated room. The machine transmits chapter XII of the approved 1909 Histoire de France. The pupil has an 1876 printing in the desk.
Altered ruleTransmitted sentences carry no source. Only paper can be cited, and each edition is wrong about something.
Actors and concernsPupil: get the answer right and know which is true. Master: keep to the 1909 edition before the inspector visits (inspector invented, see 03). Émile, the crank boy: keep his mother's pay. Lucie: trust what she heard.
ActionsPupil: listen, read, compare, speak, inspect, feed the hopper. Master: answer, discipline, call recitation. Émile: crank, stop, help.
ResistancePatience of the master; the caps that block speech; the timing of when the master looks away.
Consequence revealedWho decides what the class knows, and whose work is left out of both the room and the lesson.

What changed in the method

The case produced three kinds of revision: corrections to the brief, proposed additions to Jang's codebook, and a test that can be carried to the next artifact.

Corrections to the brief

  • "An adult operates a crank": the operator is a youth in an apron (R7). Kirkus calls him a workman; his age and role are not settled.
  • "Wired listening devices": the caps cover the crown as well as the ears (R3). Audio is one reading; Jang codes direct transmission to the brain.
  • "Commonly attributed to Villemard, circa 1910": the BnF catalogue, which holds the 1910 sheet, supplies Côté in brackets. The attribution is open.
  • This case's own first pass: seven pupils (six), "boys only" (two probable girls), master holding a book "open" over the hopper (closed, at his chest), "no electrical infrastructure" (an unexplained cable). All corrected in 01–03.

Repeatable tests

The witness test. For any speculation about transmitting knowledge: when two receivers disagree, what can they point to? If the answer is nothing, the tetrad's reverse is probably the loss of verification.

The remove-the-machine test. Erase the device and list what remains with region citations. If the remainder is a recognizable present-day institution, code its invariants before coding the device.

The crank test. Find whoever powers or maintains the apparatus. Ask whether they receive what it delivers.

The input–output test. Ask what the device's form says it does to its input (reads, copies, grinds, burns) before naming what it does for its users. The mill reading of this card was missed by skipping it.

Prompt D (untested). Starting situation: the caps write into the body, and nothing records what was received. Altered rule: a pupil cannot tell a transmitted memory from one of their own. Actors: a pupil, a parent who remembers differently. Resistance: the pupil's certainty. Consequence: whether memory can be audited.

The edition test. Name the specific content in the machine and check whether it was disputed at T0.

Playtest record

The game engine was explored exhaustively by script before publishing: 132,379 reachable states, maximum 13 moves, zero dead ends, zero actions without effect. All 6 endings and all 14 notebook observations are reachable. Of 76,587 ending states, 2 reach none of the seven assumptions; both are the fully passive path (listen to every page, recite). The mean is 4.1 assumptions per ending.

The critic played paths by hand and found four bugs in the first version, all fixed and re-tested by script: the passive path announced the contradiction; feeding the hopper was only possible after the canal page had played, so the class never heard the fed book though the ending said it did; whispering to Lucie with the cap off credited you with hearing a sentence you missed; and reciting the lesson was allowed without having heard it.

Not tested: whether a human player reaches an assumption without the debrief naming it. That is the acceptance test in the brief and needs players.

Known limit: timing forbids doing everything in one lesson. The master's back is turned only on pages 4 and 5, before the canal is read, so feeding the hopper means reading your book and befriending Émile in the first four moves. This is intended, and it also means a single play reveals only part of the case.

Independent critique

Run by a separate agent that had not seen the work being made. It viewed the image, checked sources, and played the game by hand. Summarized; its findings and what was done with each.

Agreements

Pew 2025, the 1909 bishops' letter, the 1882 condemnation of four civics manuals, the Ohio School of the Air start date and the Khanmigo pilot checked out. "Obsolesce reading, not the book" is well supported, as are the remove-the-machine and crank tests. The crank operator is plausibly a youth; HISTOIRE DE FRANCE is legible at zoom. The BnF record could not be opened by the critic either.

Strongest competing interpretation

The machine is a mill and the card is a force-feeding joke: books are ground up and pupils are stuffed. This makes the book consumed, not preserved, and makes "retrieve the lectio" less likely. Recorded as C4 with its own tetrad. It agrees with v1 that pupils no longer read; it disagrees about what is retrieved and whether the book survives the lesson.
