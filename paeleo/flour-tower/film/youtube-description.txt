THE FLOUR TOWER, HANDMADE · PAE-03 · CRASH LAB / PAELEO

In the 1930s Disney trained animators on a flour sack: no face, no limbs, no fingers. Mood has to read through weight, silhouette, the line of action and four corners that drag behind the body. The test's highest bar was a sack that thinks before it acts.

PAE-03 gives the sack a decision. A knife slices a tower of packed flour with a penny on top, the old parlor game. A half-empty flour sack flinches, notices the tower lean, comes to it head low and holds it up. The tower falls. The sack catches the penny, gathers the scattered flour back into itself, and stands where the tower stood. The knife hovers, and leaves. It is the tower of Babel told backwards: what was built and scattered is gathered into one body.

The story and a score of ten choices are held still. Six SCION swarms each built the sack, the tower and the knife from nothing in their own language, without seeing each other's work. The same sound score plays under every film.

03:51 The result

What was measured: every film is a single web page that records its own render. In all six, the ten choices land within a quarter second of the score, the sack keeps its volume through squash and stretch, and the flour is conserved between tower, pile and sack. Each film was judged by an independent critic that saw only the film and the brief; two passed outright and four passed narrowly after two or three repair rounds.

What the critics kept finding, in every material: the cause came too late (the tower did not visibly lean before the sack noticed it), the penny got tossed instead of dropping, the brace vanished or retreated, the sack went top-heavy in the leap and the final stand, and the films' own checks were kinder than the picture.
