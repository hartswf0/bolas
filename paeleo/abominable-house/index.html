The Burning of the Abominable House

After Italo Calvino’s story. A house burns with its four tenants: the widow Roessler, her adopted daughter Ogiva, the landlord Inigo and the wrestler Belindo. A copybook survives with only its index: twelve abominable deeds, and no record of who did what to whom. The tenants are flour sacks. Every deed can be done by any tenant to any other, so the copybook could have told 1212 stories. These are the films, the bank that plays any of them, and the sheets they were built from.

How it is made

  • The ledger. Every deed changes the house: marks on the body, what each tenant knows and feels, who is alive, what role the house has given them (after Barthes), and each tenant’s belief about the others (a tug-of-war update after Goodman, Tenenbaum and Gerstenberg). Each deed happens in a room, under a light, before whoever is there (after Knowles and Tenney’s House of Dust).
  • The tenants. Beliefs, desires and committed plans (after Bratman, and Rao and Georgeff), a memory stream with reflections (after Park and colleagues), and a dial between practical and symbolic reasons.
  • The watcher and the author. A model of the audience infers each tenant’s hidden plan from what it does (after Baker, Saxe and Tenenbaum); the author searches for stories that steer that inference (after Chandra and colleagues).
  • The sieve. Calvino, in “Prose and Anticombinatorics,” used the computer to throw stories away. The film bank counts the consistent ones exactly, and keeps the best story of each kind in an archive named for his Six Memos.
  • The films. Every frame is drawn in the browser from code, captured headless and encoded; the sound is synthesized grain by grain.
