The Burning of the Abominable House of Flour

Watching code, judging possibilities, and making films with language models

Abstract. In 1973 Italo Calvino described a computer programmer confronted with 8.9 trillion possible assignments of twelve deeds to four characters. His collaboration with Paul Braffort sought to eliminate inadmissible combinations, yet his fictional programmer changes a rule after imagining a scene. I revisit this problem through PAE-04: The Burning of the Abominable House, an executable edition in which four flour sacks perform the deeds. Rather than asking an LLM for video pixels, I use language models to author procedural animation programs, execute those programs, and inspect their rendered performances. I distinguish three judgments in the passage from a combinatorial space to a film: what to exclude, what to select, and how to stage it. Exact counts show that exclusions concerning one character alter the modeled insurance liability of another. Matched cinematic realizations and a caption-free perceptual protocol expose a further problem: whether the differences recorded in a computational model can be made legible through animation. The latter remains an open empirical question. The project proposes staging, including the deliberate withholding of a human body, as a site of literary interpretation rather than a final step in the illustration of a plot.

Keywords: anticombinatorics; computational literature; procedural cinema; generative AI; animation; interpretive modeling

1. Introduction

Four tenants die in a fire. A charred copybook survives, but all that can be read of it is a list of twelve abominable deeds. Skiller, an insurer, commissions Waldemar, a programmer, to establish who did what to whom. The assignment matters to the insurance company: a death may invalidate a policy depending on who caused it (Calvino 1995 [1973], 156–169). In “The Burning of the Abominable House,” the writer confronts the computer with a finite set of relations and a practically unmanageable number of combinations.

The problem looks different today. A language model can generate a detective story almost as quickly as it can describe one. A procedural system can assign actors to Calvino's twelve deeds without writing out every resulting history. Neither operation determines which story deserves a reader's or spectator's time. Calvino's experiment is therefore worth returning to not because it anticipated contemporary generative AI, but because its incompletion reveals problems that inexpensive generation makes more pressing.

I distinguish three judgments: exclusion, selection and staging. Each can be delegated in part to computation, and each can become the subject of an interpretive act. I demonstrate this distinction through an executable reconstruction of Calvino's house in which four faceless flour sacks carry the roles of the four tenants. The title is literal as well as metaphorical: flour is sifted, the flour sack is an animation exercise, and the house burns. The computational edition makes a literary sieve available to the spectator through film.

In the first film, the blackened notebook appears against a field of rubble; pale particles rise from the ruined frame. The four characters are first a diagram, then bodies in rooms. The multiplication becomes part of the film's temporal organization: the viewer is made to wait for the number to accumulate before the house is reconstituted. The same program that stages the multiplication stages the characters' differences. This is the initial form of the paper's argument: the numerical problem has to be made sensible.

2. Sifting: a rule is a reading

In Calvino's 1967 lecture “Cybernetics and Ghosts,” the literary machine might eventually depart from its own conventions. Duncan (2012) traces the changing role of authorial deviation in Calvino's subsequent thinking. By the time Calvino and Braffort presented their work in 1977, the computer had a more circumscribed task: reducing a space of possible relations through compatibility rules (Braffort 1997, §2–3; Calvino 2003 [1981]). The contrast is useful today. A system that samples a learned distribution and a system that explicitly excludes a configuration do not perform the same act. An exclusion can be named, tested and withdrawn. A probability, in itself, states no such rule.

In the present edition, I fix the order of the twelve headings in the English copybook and allow each to be assigned any of the twelve ordered pairs of distinct tenants. Each casting has a base-12 address. There are 12¹² = 8,916,100,448,256 such addresses, not 8.9 trillion independently produced or reviewed films. Indexing makes a possible casting cheap to specify; realizing it in motion and attending to it are separate operations. The Address Map makes the space navigable without storing every film.

Calvino's fictional programmer treats the wrestler's strength and his mode of fighting as grounds for exclusion. These are judgments about a character, not necessary laws. Following McCarty (2004), I make the exclusions executable in order to examine their effects. The policy calculator lets a reader change them individually. Its five-rule preset disallows Belindo knifing, Belindo being strangled, his being tied without a gun threat against him, a slanderer also snooping on or blackmailing the same person, and knifing and strangling the same victim. The preset is an authored interpretation, not a faithful reconstruction of every constraint in Braffort's program.

The widow's policy illustrates why the difference matters. Under the model's settings of certain fatality for the relevant attacks and no prior probability of a planted index, the life policy is void if Ogiva knifes or strangles Roessler. With equal weighting across retained castings, the unrestricted payment share is (11/12)² = 121/144. The wrestler restrictions name neither woman, yet their introduction changes the relative prevalence of the voiding assignments. The share falls to 64/81. All five rules leave 50/63. These are model-dependent fractions of assignments, not actual crime probabilities or observed payments.

3. Selecting: a probability is not a preference

After sifting, the filmmaker still faces more than two and a half trillion addresses. This resembles Borges's library: making everything addressable does not make it worth reading. Calvino's later account of the clinamen is particularly useful here. The aesthetically motivated departure from a rule is not equivalent to a random deviation or a low-probability sample (Duncan 2012). It presupposes an alternative that could have been chosen and a reason to prefer the departure. A temperature parameter alone supplies no such reason.

The edition separates the hard sieve from a character-based generator. Its utilities depend on practical and symbolic motives, beliefs, previous events and intentions; a seeded sampler chooses among the weighted pairings at each step (agents.js). These preferences are authored and adjustable. They are not empirical estimates of real human conduct and do not establish aesthetic value. More importantly, the films can be interrogated independently of their scores. In a documented public screening candidate, Roessler is assigned an action while bound by a previous deed. The ledger records a contradiction even though the five-rule preset accepts the address. Of its 132 single-deed recastings, fifteen clear that contradiction. This is a search result, not a finding that those fifteen are better films (CL-03–04).

Consider choosing a version because a character's later action would make an earlier scene ironic, rather than because each action is independently credible. Such a selection depends on the unfolding experience of a viewer. A plausibility score can inform it but does not determine it. In this sense the system retrieves the author's problem from Calvino's later account. It is not a shortage of permutations but an unresolved question of taste. A good film might require revising an exclusion, rejecting a favored candidate, or inventing a form in which a previously implausible event can be understood.

The two screenings also differ in editing and pace. The random film and the selected film therefore do not constitute a controlled human preference comparison. They do make the question available: what is being chosen when a film is picked, and what is being changed when that film is cut? In the software, the author can seek irony, suspense or a twist rather than maximize believability. In crossed.js, the modeled audience updates hypotheses about characters' intentions; a separate search scores candidate sequences for the chosen narrative device, with penalties and a believability term. This is inspired by Chandra et al. (2024). It is a model of a watcher, not an observation of an actual watcher.

4. Staging: making a judgment visible

The language model is not the projection apparatus. It is commissioned to construct one. In the deed bank, an animation module accepts time, actor, victim and shared rendering context. The browser executes its JavaScript against a Canvas surface. State and consequences are calculated separately; the same address can be replayed or inspected without another LLM request. The film bank makes this division especially visible through WATCH RENDER, the live layer-compositing view, and through the guide that links an explanation to the actual code.

Hannes Bajohr (2024) calls the causal production of images by digital text operative ekphrasis. Here that operation becomes recursive: a description produces a program, the program produces moving images, and an inspection of those images produces a new description of what needs repair. The prompt is not the final artwork or even necessarily a specification of one finished shot. It commissions a procedure that can be exercised across several castings.

Watching code

The project treats the LLM as a producer of software, not as a renderer. Its task is to read an animation brief, construct a formal mechanism, and return runnable source. The browser executes the code and generates the audiovisual surface. A separate observer can then inspect that surface and return criticism to the builder. In the final artwork, the program need not call a language model when a viewer requests a film. The distinction between the expensive act of constructing the apparatus and the recurring act of operating it is part of the research method.

The precursor Flour Tower supplied six independently authored animation implementations with a common 32-second score. It required the systems to report geometry and timing and made the rendered frames available for criticism. The seventeen logged critic rounds are particularly instructive. In SCION 02, round 1, the automated checks marked each row as holding, but the critic inspecting frames reported a headless-looking silhouette in the brace interval and an almost invisible tower lean. Round 2 reported that the headless pose had disappeared and the lean had become visible, yet also found an unexpected problem in the narrow mobile layout. See the actual critique archive. A numerical gate could not settle how a movement read. Consider the first Reiniger critique: it sampled the 32-second film at half-second intervals and inspected denser windows, reporting 375 frames. The brace was a numerical success because a height threshold had been crossed, yet its neck rotated so abruptly that the object appeared headless. The tower's change in angle was measurable but not visible enough to motivate the character's response. In the second critique the visual correction was reported alongside a new failure: a 513-pixel canvas in a 390-pixel mobile viewport cropped the right part of the performance. The lesson is methodological, not a benchmark score. The machine's description of its own work and the machine's reading of the rendered work are not interchangeable. A critic who sees the source's label before the frame can inherit the intended interpretation. The archive therefore distinguishes picture-first observations from numerical verification; it does not establish a fully blinded human study. Nor is an agent critic a replacement for a human audience: its verdicts are situated interpretations recorded in a process archive.

Each animation lineage is more than a look. In the actual Deed IV source, Peter Foldès's drawn metamorphosis is interpreted as a five-segment, ninety-point contour correspondence. The four sacks have distinct key contours. This makes changes in silhouette a consequence of explicit geometry and timing rather than an unstable visual resemblance between independently generated clips. The critic can identify a frame, a designer can revise its generative procedure, and the same revision can propagate across the twelve pairings.

In the story Waldemar withdraws a prohibition after imagining a scene involving what first seemed an impossible sequence of events (Calvino 1995, 161). The image changes what he considers admissible. The movement from literary rule to cinematic imagination is therefore not unidirectional. A film may expose a problem with a rule, or make an excluded sequence newly intelligible. I treat animation as a way to investigate such judgments, rather than as illustration after the literary analysis is complete.

The edition draws on the flour-sack exercise in animation training (Thomas and Johnston 1981, 24–27). In place of the human figure, I use four differently colored sacks, whose weight, silhouette, timing, movement and relation to objects must carry the performance. The deed bank organizes twelve actions over twelve possible ordered pairings: 144 cells assembled from 96 individual role configurations. The full film combines procedural deed, transition, consequence and memory modules. Seventeen recorded critic rounds belong to the flour-sack precursor, PAE-03; they are evidence of iterative making, not a substitute for independent audience evaluation.

The matched recasting (Figure 4) demonstrates that a change to one assignment can change both the performance and its financial interpretation. It is not a controlled test of rendering alone: the actor differs. For the separate question of perceptual legibility, I use VTX-0.1, a two-sack lamp-room experiment. It changes a single authored goal from assisting to deliberate tipping. The two performances are reported to be identical through frame 77 (at 24 fps, approximately 3.21 seconds), then diverge. The caption-free trial asks a spectator to identify which action is deliberate. It separates the intended meaning in the scene record from the meaning inferred by a viewer.

The project records differences in state and animation. It does not yet establish that viewers interpret them as intended. The ballot randomizes left/right presentation and withholds explanatory labels before a response is locked. The project claims ledger marks this question open (C13); there were no committed human ballots in that record. Staging is demonstrably another authoring operation, but its success as communication remains to be tested. This limit is central to the paper. A computationally certified action is not yet an intelligible performance.

Geertz's distinction between a twitch and a wink (1973, 6–7) makes this a problem of interpretation rather than a problem of pose estimation alone. A physical movement can be identical in several circumstances and nevertheless amount to different actions. In the lamp-room comparison, the authored goals and causal state differ. Whether a spectator can infer that difference from the animation is still an open question. The caption-free viewer makes a modest version of the question testable.

5. What the sack will not show

Calvino's list includes sexual violence and other forms of harm. A literal adaptation with photorealistic people would require decisions that a generative system's ability to produce images cannot settle. I chose not to simulate those acts on human likenesses. For deed VIII, the performance moves behind a curtain; the event can still have consequences in the ledger, and the viewer encounters its traces. The curtain is therefore a formal device rather than an absence of technique. The actual Deed VIII module builds separate RGBA layers for the actor, recipient and background. The curtain functions as a holdout matte, removing the obscured parts of the character layers before compositing. Sound also withholds the hidden action: the cloth and the reopening provide temporal boundaries. This is a concrete implementation of the decision not to show, not an appeal to an external content filter. A film may communicate that something has happened without displaying the act itself.

Withholding the body does not eliminate the ethical difficulty of representing harm. It can obscure the embodied and social dimensions of violence; the ledger's reductive headings should not be mistaken for an exhaustive account of suffering. But the choice makes visible a distinction between generating a spectacle and making an interpretive representation. An author can deliberately restrict the means of showing an event without pretending that it does not occur in the fictional world. The method is specific to this literary and artistic problem, not a general claim that all sensitive narratives should be abstracted.

In this method, the decision to withhold is not a substitute for responsibility: it is a representational decision subject to criticism. It must be possible to inspect the state transitions and to see how the apparatus handles a subject that it does not depict directly. The abstraction is limited, deliberate, and grounded in this particular adaptation.

6. Conclusion

The history of Calvino's machine suggests that the abundance of combinations is not the abundance of literature. An address is cheap to specify. A filter can retain trillions of possibilities. A plausibility model can rank or sample them. None determines how a spectator will read a particular gesture, or whether the gesture should be shown.

The contribution of the house of flour is to separate these judgments and bring them into contact. The policy calculation makes the effects of a literary interpretation inspectable. The film bank offers a way to experience and challenge a casting. The perceptual protocol exposes the remaining distinction between the intention encoded by an author and the interpretation made by a viewer. The last requires further evidence.

In 1973, Calvino's programmer imagined an image that made him reconsider his sieve. The contemporary literary machine should make room for the same movement in reverse: a viewer encounters a film and is able to return to the rules and ask what made it possible. Anticombinatorics is not finished when the possibilities have been reduced. It continues in how they are made to appear.

Research materials and limitations

The executable edition is PAE-04. Its evidence ledger, replay script, claims audit, field guide and flour-sack precursor are linked directly. The reported results are derived from the repository's recorded evidence; no new participant data are claimed. Historical page references are those in the project bibliography. Drafting and revision were assisted by a language model. At runtime the cited procedural edition executes browser code rather than requesting generated clips from an LLM. Historical citations should be checked against the relevant print editions before submission. No human audience evaluation, token-usage benchmark, or comparative commercial-video experiment is claimed.

B. Consequences: what remains after a deed

Each event has a corresponding consequence module. The sheets allow the reader to compare what is left behind with the event that produced it. A physical trace, a restraint, a displaced prop or a changed character is part of the following state; the artwork's consistency can be examined against the separate ledger. The state is an authored model and not a complete representation of the fictional characters' lives.

C. Memory: four partial histories

Four memory modules translate prior events into different visual vocabularies. The ledger records who has knowledge of a deed; the agent system retrieves relevant records. Neither entails that a character remembers everything or remembers it truthfully. Compare the source's memory machinery with the sheets below.

D. Transitions: joining the house

Thirteen transition modules bridge deed units, including the final movement toward the fire. These are necessary because separate clips do not automatically make a world. H. Perceptual protocol and data status

The two-sack lamp-room experiment is an invented minimal adaptation, not a recovered Calvino scene. Its paired cases are related by a change to one authored goal, with shared initial conditions and explicit downstream traces. The independent viewer randomizes the presentation and withholds the labels. The original package includes rejected and repaired versions, a deterministic validator and caption-free exports. The wider three-trial ballot includes two further cases from the four-sack edition.

Status: the checked claims ledger (C13) records no committed human ballots. The claim that a spectator can identify the intended distinction is therefore untested. The protocol also does not measure which film is more interesting.
